Synthesis of technical patterns across the four search themes
A few patterns recur across the 2022–2025 window:

Matching structures in NOMA pairing and subcarrier assignment.
Recent works still frequently reduce parts of NOMA resource assignment to a (many-to-one / many-to-many) matching game or an assignment problem solved by the Hungarian method. This shows up from classic “two-sided matching” formulations (users ↔ subchannels) to modern RIS-aided multi-cluster NOMA where Hungarian pairing coexists with alternating optimization and SCA for continuous variables. 

Swap/exchange stability as a practical heuristic in coupled radio–compute allocation.
In hybrid NOMA–MEC offloading, one common pattern is: (i) obtain near-closed-form or convexified solutions for power/time (e.g., via KKT/SCA), then (ii) solve discrete subchannel assignment with an exchange-stable / swap-style algorithm (often modeled as a 3D assignment), explicitly discussing convergence and energy-efficiency gains. 

MEC-offloading matching is trending toward “online” variants, but often not in grouped NOMA settings.
Some MEC papers use “online matching” ideas (sometimes with digital twin context) for dynamic environments; others incorporate uncertainty (e.g., intermittent links, unknown CPU cycles) and propose repeated matching-like mechanisms. These are closest to your need for slot-level decisions, but they typically do not combine all your target ingredients (Grouped NOMA + multi-UAV co-channel interference + burst traffic). 

Multi-UAV NOMA grouping is often clustering-first, optimization-second.
Multi-UAV NOMA papers frequently choose clusters using k-means / spectral clustering / matching (max-weight matching), then optimize trajectories/resource scheduling. This is valuable if your scenario needs stable grouping, but many papers assume mobility planning over an “episode” (offline) rather than strict per-slot online constraints.

Implications for your target system design
A realistic “2 fixed UAV + grouped (MC-)NOMA + cross-UAV co-channel interference + burst traffic + per-slot online policy” tends to require three layers that most papers only partially cover:

Interference coupling across UAVs (multi-transmitter co-channel)
Multi-UAV papers explicitly modeling co-channel interference are the closest starting point for your two-UAV interference geometry and coordination constraints. Multi-UAV deployment/trajectory works often contain the interference terms you would need, even if their optimization horizon is longer than one slot. 

Grouped NOMA primitives that are simple enough for per-slot decisions
P26 (two-users-per-subcarrier MC-NOMA) is a clean, implementable template for “grouped NOMA.” P28 and P29 also provide hybrid access knobs (NOMA/OMA switching, time/power co-optimization) that can be repurposed when burst traffic makes strict NOMA always-on suboptimal. 

Online pairing/offloading control
For “online per slot,” the clearest conceptual bridges are: (i) bandit-style pairing (P20) and (ii) online matching views in UAV-MEC (P10) plus uncertainty-aware MEC formulations (P11). These are not complete solutions for your full radio model, but they provide reusable algorithmic scaffolding (reward design, stability notions, exploration vs exploitation, and maintaining feasibility).
